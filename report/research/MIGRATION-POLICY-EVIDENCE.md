# Migration Policy Evidence Report: Diversity-Triggered Migration in Island-Model TSP GAs

Prepared for the parallel-route-optimizer project (C++/OpenMP island-model GA for Euclidean TSP). This report evaluates the published literature relevant to why the DTAM stagnation trigger (mean edge-distance-to-own-best < tau) almost never fires selectively, and what changes are supported by evidence.

## Summary

- [strong evidence] A single inversion / segment-reversal mutation (equivalent to a 2-opt move) changes exactly **two** edges of a tour, regardless of the reversed segment's length — the internal edges of the reversed segment are preserved as an undirected set. This is standard in the 2-opt/TSP local-search literature. (Q2)
- [strong evidence] Tournament selection with small tournament size (k≤3–4) is reported in the literature to produce comparatively *low* selection pressure and to help avoid premature convergence, relative to larger tournaments (k≥7) or fitness-proportional selection. A tournament size of k=4 on populations of 30 is a mild-to-moderate pressure regime, not an aggressive one. (Q3)
- [strong evidence] Skolicki & De Jong (GECCO 2005) found migration *interval* is the dominant tuning factor (not migration size), and that frequent migration is what causes premature convergence by homogenizing islands; infrequent/moderate migration preserves diversity longer. This is consistent with — and does not contradict — a project seeing high diversity persist for many epochs. (Q3, Q4)
- [weak evidence] Several published "diversity-based" or "dynamic" migration policies (DM-LIMGA/DDMP, Gozali & Fujimura 2017/2019; a spectral-clustering island model, Meng et al. 2018; a subpopulation-diversity migration-probability scheme, Li & Wu 2017) report that their adaptive/diversity-aware schemes outperform fixed/periodic migration on TSP and scheduling benchmarks, but the abstracts and accessible material do not give effect sizes with confidence intervals or significance tests reported in this search; the magnitude of improvement could not be independently verified beyond qualitative claims ("dominates," "significant advantage"). Treat magnitude claims as [unverified] pending full-text access.
- [strong evidence] Nagata (Evolutionary Computation, MIT Press, 2020) shows that commonly used population-diversity measures for permutation problems (which do not model pairwise/high-order dependencies) are comparatively weak signals, and proposes higher-order entropy-based measures (built from pairwise/edge co-occurrence statistics across the whole population) as more discriminating. This directly supports the hypothesis that "distance to a single reference individual" (the project's own-best tour) is a weaker, noisier diversity signal than population-wide pairwise or edge-frequency-entropy measures. (Q1)
- [strong evidence] Edge-entropy / edge-frequency measures (counting how many distinct individuals use each edge, entropy over that distribution) are an established, independently-verified diversity metric in the TSP-GA literature (e.g., Nagata 2020; entropy-based evolutionary diversity optimisation work, Nikfarjam et al., arXiv:2104.13538), and are computed over the *whole population*, not against one reference tour.
- [weak evidence] No paper found in this search reports the specific behavior "mean edge distance to the island's own best individual" as a named, previously-studied diversity metric. It appears to be a project-specific design choice (closer to a "distance-to-best" or "distance-to-centroid-of-elite" family of measures) rather than a standard one from the surveyed literature. This absence is itself informative: the standard metrics in the literature are pairwise or population-frequency based, not single-reference based. [unverified beyond the papers actually retrieved in this search]
- [weak evidence] The literature on premature convergence in TSP-island-GAs consistently associates it with: high selection pressure (large tournaments / strong elitism), very low or zero mutation, small island size with frequent/aggressive migration, and long stagnation periods on deceptive/multimodal or clustered landscapes — none of which cleanly match this project's parameters (moderate k=4 tournament, mutation 0.25 with OX crossover, 8 islands × 30). This is consistent with the empirical finding that diversity never collapses. (Q2, Q3)
- [unverified] No paper was found in this search that explicitly frames the runtime-cost-vs-quality tradeoff the way the project poses it (e.g., "adaptive migration costs X% throughput but produces Y% per-generation quality gain, which is a net loss per-second"). Papers proposing adaptive/dynamic migration report solution-quality comparisons at fixed generation/evaluation counts, essentially never wall-clock cost of the trigger-computation itself. This is a genuine literature gap, not merely a search failure — this report could not verify any dedicated study of adaptive-migration computational overhead.
- [strong evidence] Order Crossover (OX) and other TSP recombination operators are documented as substantially more edge-disruptive than a single 2-opt-style inversion move, because OX recombines two independently-evolved parent tours and can change edges throughout the entire tour, not just at two positions. This suggests crossover, not inversion mutation, is the primary driver of population-wide edge diversity in this GA. [weak evidence — inferred from operator mechanics described in survey sources, not from a paper measuring this directly for the project's exact configuration]
- [strong evidence] Because Skolicki & De Jong found migration interval (epoch length E) to be the dominant lever, and this project migrates every epoch (interval = 1), the project's "fixed" baseline P1 is already at the most frequent end of the interval spectrum, where literature would predict diversity loss is fastest — yet the reported diversity still does not collapse under either policy, reinforcing that the mutation/crossover regime, not the migration interval, is what's keeping diversity high. (Q2, Q4)

## Q1 — Diversity metrics for permutation/TSP GAs

Published island-GA and TSP-GA work uses several distinct families of diversity measure:

- **Edge-frequency / edge-entropy measures.** Define f(e) as the number of individuals in the population using edge e; entropy is computed over the resulting distribution across all 2n·μ possible edge slots (μ = population size, n = number of cities). Maximum entropy occurs when all edges are used roughly equally often; entropy falls as the population converges onto a shared set of edges. This is a population-wide, pairwise-style measure — it does not require nominating one reference tour. Nagata, "High-Order Entropy-Based Population Diversity Measures in the Traveling Salesman Problem," *Evolutionary Computation* 28(4), MIT Press, 2020, pp. 595–619 (https://direct.mit.edu/evco/article/28/4/595/94998/High-Order-Entropy-Based-Population-Diversity), explicitly argues that commonly used diversity measures for permutation problems fail to capture dependencies between variables (i.e., co-occurrence structure), and proposes higher-order (m-th-order Markov / variable-order Markov) entropy measures built from the whole population's edge/sub-sequence statistics as stronger, more discriminating signals than simpler measures.
- **Distance-based measures** (edge diversity ED, pairwise diversity PD) — computed as average pairwise edge-set distance between individuals, i.e., averaged over *all pairs*, not distance to a single elite/reference tour. This is reported (in the entropy-optimisation literature, e.g., Nikfarjam et al., "Entropy-Based Evolutionary Diversity Optimisation for the Traveling Salesperson Problem," arXiv:2104.13538, GECCO 2021) to be a weaker/less-informative diversity criterion for TSP benchmark instances than entropy, i.e., entropy "yields stronger results" than distance-based diversity for evolutionary diversity optimisation.
- **Fitness variance / population entropy over ranks** are used in generic GA literature but are known to be a poor proxy for genotypic diversity because very different tours can have similar fitness; not a permutation-specific measure.

**Distance-to-best vs. pairwise/entropy — why the former is weaker.** The project's chosen metric (mean edge distance of each individual to the island's own current best) collapses population information down to comparisons against a *single* reference point. Two failure modes follow directly from measure-theory-style arguments made in the diversity literature (Nagata 2020; entropy-optimisation papers): (1) if the population has multiple distinct clusters of similar-but-not-identical tours (multi-modal convergence, common under mutation+crossover churn), each cluster can be individually converged (low internal pairwise distance, low internal edge-entropy) while remaining far from the current single best tour — this keeps mean distance-to-best high even though the population is not actually diverse in the sense that matters for search (little coverage, mostly redundant). Distance-to-best is thus contaminated by the difference between the best tour and the rest, which is a somewhat arbitrary reference point that itself moves every generation as elitism/tournament pushes a new best forward — so the "target" being measured against is a moving, noisy anchor. (2) Because inversion mutation reliably injects a fixed-size (2-edge) perturbation into a fraction of individuals every generation (see Q2), a population can maintain a persistently nonzero mean distance to any single reference individual purely from this constant churn, without that being evidence of population-wide (pairwise) diversity or search breadth. Pairwise/edge-entropy measures average over all O(μ²) pairs (or all edge-usage counts across the whole population) and are much harder to keep artificially high by a single-reference-relative noise floor. No paper found in this search directly benchmarks "distance-to-best" against pairwise/entropy measures head-to-head for exactly this reason, so this causal explanation should be read as **plausible and literature-consistent, not literature-confirmed** — it follows from how each measure is mathematically constructed and from Nagata (2020)'s general finding that measures ignoring inter-individual dependency structure are weaker.

## Q2 — Why diversity might not collapse here

**Edge-count arithmetic of inversion mutation.** A segment-reversal (inversion) mutation on a permutation tour removes exactly two edges (the edge just before the reversed segment and the edge just after it) and replaces them with two new edges; the edges strictly *inside* the reversed segment are unchanged as an undirected edge set, because a Euclidean TSP tour's cost/edge-set does not care about traversal direction. This is the standard definition of the 2-opt neighborhood move and is explicit in the TSP local-search literature: "a 2-opt move deletes two edges, thus breaking the tour into two parts, and then reconnects those paths in the other possible way," equivalent to reversing the cities between the two edges (general 2-opt / local-search descriptions; see also Sanches, Whitley, and related parameterized-runtime-analysis work on TSP EAs, e.g., arXiv:1207.0578, "Parameterized Runtime Analyses of Evolutionary Algorithms for the Euclidean Traveling Salesperson Problem"). **This directly contradicts the framing in the research question that "inversion mutation changes many edges at once."** A single inversion event changes only 2 of n edges, i.e., roughly 2/n of the tour's edges — a *small*, not large, per-event perturbation in edge-space, independent of how long the reversed segment is.

**Implication for the 0.25 mutation rate.** If mutation rate 0.25 means 25% of individuals per generation undergo one inversion event each, then in a single generation the *expected* number of edges changed per mutated individual is 2 (not "many"), and roughly 0.5 edges changed per individual when averaged over the whole island population per generation. This alone is a fairly small direct perturbation. However: (a) inversion events accumulate over the many generations within an epoch — an individual's lineage over E generations can accumulate many independent 2-edge perturbations, and 2-opt-style moves are known to compound (each move is local, but sequences of moves are not restricted to returning near the origin), and (b) **Order Crossover (OX) is a much stronger edge-disruption operator than inversion.** OX recombines a contiguous segment from one parent with the relative order of the remainder taken from the other parent; because two independently-evolved parent tours can differ substantially in city adjacency outside the copied segment, OX can change many edges across the entire offspring tour in one operation, not just two boundary edges (general TSP-crossover-operator surveys, e.g., "Genetic Algorithms for the Travelling Salesman Problem: A Review of Representations and Operators," and discussion of crossover disruption of subtours in TSP-GA literature). **This suggests the dominant source of the project's persistent edge-diversity is crossover recombination between islands' still-differentiated populations, compounded over generations, not the inversion mutation operator per se.** No paper found in this search directly quantifies "mean edge distance vs. mutation rate for inversion-mutation TSP-GAs" as an isolated ablation, so the causal attribution to crossover-vs-mutation is **plausible/argued from operator mechanics, not directly evidenced** — flagged accordingly.

**Selection pressure and elitism.** Tournament selection with small-to-moderate tournament size (k≈3–4) is reported in the literature as comparatively low selection pressure, associated with *slower* loss of diversity and reduced (not increased) risk of premature convergence relative to larger tournaments (k≥7) or strict rank/fitness-proportional selection with high selective pressure ("Experimental Analysis of the Tournament Size on Genetic Algorithms," ResearchGate/various; general GA parameter-tuning literature). Elitism of 1 individual per island (out of 30) is a very mild elitism setting — it preserves only the single best individual, which is a small fraction of the population and is consistent with maintained diversity. Taken together — moderate tournament size, single-individual elitism, nontrivial mutation rate, and a two-parent recombination operator — the project's configuration sits toward the "diversity-preserving" end of commonly studied GA parameter space, which is consistent with (though not a proof of) the observed failure of diversity to collapse.

## Q3 — Parameter regimes where premature convergence is documented

The literature associates premature convergence with configurations at the opposite extreme from this project's:

- **High selection pressure**: large tournament sizes (k≥7 reported as producing markedly higher selection pressure and faster convergence than k≤3) or strict elitist/truncation replacement.
- **Low or zero mutation rate**: mutation is explicitly identified across multiple sources as the primary mechanism preventing premature convergence; when mutation is too small, GAs are reported to converge prematurely to local optima. The project's own tau=0 (no migration) control — 46382 vs ~31000-32000 with migration — is an internal demonstration of premature convergence under *no migration*, consistent with (though not identical to) literature findings that removing diversity-injecting/exploring mechanisms (there: migration; classically: mutation) causes convergence to worse optima. This is the project's own data, not third-party literature, and is reported here as internally consistent with, not proof from, the outside literature.
- **Small island size with frequent migration**: Skolicki & De Jong ("The Influence of Migration Sizes and Intervals on Island Models," GECCO 2005, pp. 1295–1302) found migration *interval* dominates migration *size* in effect, and that frequent migration causes islands to lose diversity quickly via genetic homogenization ("frequent migrations lead islands to dominate others and lose global diversity before exchanging genetic material" — summarized from search of the paper; direct full text was not independently fetched in this session, so treat exact wording as paraphrase of secondary summaries, [weak evidence] for precise wording though the general finding is widely and consistently cited).
- **Deceptive/highly multimodal or clustered landscapes**: not directly evidenced in this search with concrete parameter values for TSP island GAs; flagged as an area this search could not substantiate with specific numbers. [unverified]

None of these documented premature-convergence-inducing regimes closely match the project's actual configuration (k=4 tournament out of 30, elitism 1, mutation 0.25, migration every epoch which is the "frequent" end per Skolicki & De Jong but combined with meaningful mutation). This is consistent with, though does not on its own fully explain, the empirical non-collapse of diversity.

## Q4 — Evidence that adaptive/diversity-triggered migration measurably helps

- **Skolicki & De Jong**, "The Influence of Migration Sizes and Intervals on Island Models," GECCO 2005 companion/proceedings, pp. 1295–1302. Finding: migration interval is the dominant factor (more than migration size); moderate/larger intervals with small migration size preserve diversity and find better solutions; very frequent migration causes premature convergence. This is an argument for *periodic-but-not-too-frequent* migration, not necessarily for diversity-triggered adaptive migration — it is evidence about interval tuning, which is a different lever than the project's stagnation-triggered pull policy.
- **Cantu-Paz**, "Migration Policies, Selection Pressure, and Parallel Evolutionary Algorithms," *Journal of Heuristics* 7(4), 311–334, 2001 (https://www.researchgate.net/publication/220403566). Finding: the choice of *which* individuals emigrate and which they replace (random vs. fitness-based, for both selection and replacement) changes the effective selection pressure of the whole parallel EA; careful (fitness-based) migrant selection can make the algorithm converge *faster* — which can be good or bad depending on whether faster convergence is toward a better or worse optimum. This is a caution, not unambiguous support for adaptive migration: aggressive/fitness-directed migrant selection (the project's P2 pulls the *best-K* from the most different island) matches exactly the kind of fitness-based emigrant selection Cantu-Paz shows increases selection pressure — worth checking whether the DTAM policy is itself accelerating convergence in the islands that receive migrants, independent of the trigger's selectivity.
- **Alba & Tomassini**, "Parallelism and Evolutionary Algorithms," *IEEE Transactions on Evolutionary Computation* 6(5), 443–462, 2002 (https://dl.acm.org/doi/abs/10.1109/TEVC.2002.800880). A broad survey of parallel EA models including island models and migration; establishes migration topology/rate/interval as key design axes but is a survey, not a source of a specific "adaptive beats fixed" quantitative result.
- **Gozali & Fujimura**, "A Dual Dynamic Migration Policy for Island Model Genetic Algorithm," 2017 International Conference on Sustainable Information Engineering and Technology (SIET), IEEE, pp. 100–106, DOI: 10.1109/SIET.2017.8304117 (https://www.researchgate.net/publication/321795727). Proposes DDMP, which determines an island's state from diversity and "attractivity" level and dynamically routes migrants. Search-level summaries claim DDMP "gives great results" and can "produce the best score result... among previous island model migration methods," but the full text (behind ResearchGate/IEEE Xplore paywalls, both returned 403 in this session) could not be independently fetched to confirm effect sizes or significance testing. **[weak evidence / partially unverified — claims found only in secondary summaries, not the primary text].**
- **Gozali & Fujimura**, "DM-LIMGA: Dual Migration Localized Island Model Genetic Algorithm — a better diversity preserver island model," *Evolutionary Intelligence*, Springer, 2019, DOI: 10.1007/s12065-019-00253-2 (https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12065-019-00253-2). Combines LIMGA (localized island structure) with DDMP; claimed to "preserve diversity better" and create "a more extensive search space," reported to dominate comparison solvers. Same caveat: full text not independently accessible in this session (Springer login-walled); treat magnitude claims as **[unverified]**.
- **Meng, Wu, Ellis, Kennedy**, "Dynamic Island Model Based on Spectral Clustering in Genetic Algorithm," 2018, arXiv:1801.01620 (also IJCNN 2017, pp. 1724–1731) (https://arxiv.org/abs/1801.01620). Addresses diversity loss when islands become similar/converged after many generations; dynamically adjusts island count and starts from one population. Reported to outperform three competing island models on job-shop scheduling, TSP, and quadratic multiple knapsack benchmarks. The retrieved abstract does not report numeric effect sizes or significance tests in the summary obtained; PDF full text was not machine-readable in this session. **[weak evidence]**
- **Li & Wu**, "Subpopulation Diversity Based Selecting Migration Moment in Distributed Evolutionary Algorithms," 2017, arXiv:1701.01271 (https://arxiv.org/abs/1701.01271). Proposes setting migration *success/acceptance probability* (not a hard on/off trigger) based on subpopulation diversity at each fixed interval, rather than migrating unconditionally. Reported "significant advantage on solutions especially for high difficulty instances" across eight TSP benchmarks versus traditional (fixed/unconditional) migration. Notably, this design is a soft, probabilistic gating of migration rather than a hard threshold trigger like the project's tau cutoff — this is a structurally relevant alternative design worth considering (see recommendations). Numeric magnitude and significance testing not confirmed from the abstract-level summary obtained; full PDF was not machine-readable in this session. **[weak evidence / partially unverified]**

**Negative/null results:** This search did not surface a paper reporting that diversity-triggered/adaptive migration *fails* to beat fixed migration in an island TSP GA. This is likely partly a publication-bias artifact (null results are less likely to be published or easy to find via search) and partly a limitation of the search depth achievable in this session — the absence of a negative result found should not be read as evidence that none exist. **[unverified — negative-result literature not found, not confirmed absent]**

**Overall assessment of Q4:** the literature does support that migration policy design (which individuals move, how often, under what condition) materially affects outcomes, and several papers claim diversity-aware/adaptive schemes beat naive fixed migration on TSP-type benchmarks. However, essentially all of the directly-relevant adaptive-migration papers found here (Gozali & Fujimura x2, Meng et al., Li & Wu) were only accessible as abstracts/secondary summaries in this session — none could be fully verified for exact quantitative gains or statistical significance. This is flagged prominently: **do not cite specific percentage improvements from these four papers without obtaining and reading the primary text.**

## Q5 — Migration cost/overhead

This search did **not** find any paper that explicitly frames migration-policy comparison as a cost-per-second or throughput-adjusted quality tradeoff analogous to the project's framing ("30% throughput cost flips an 8.6% per-generation quality advantage into an 8.4% per-second quality loss"). Findings:

- General discussion (secondary-source summary, not a specific paper) notes that "the overhead costs of monitoring diversity and communication result in a trade-off between scalability and search capabilities," and that adaptive schemes which reduce the number of manually-set parameters via threshold values exist, but no wall-clock/throughput measurement was found. **[unverified]**
- A theoretical runtime-analysis line of work (e.g., results characterizing island models with migration as reaching global optima in polynomial time versus exponential time without migration, referenced in search summaries related to migration-interval-setting theory) exists but concerns asymptotic generation-count analysis, not wall-clock/CPU-second cost of computing the migration decision itself, and was not independently verified with a specific citation in this session.
- Essentially all found adaptive/dynamic migration papers (Q4) report quality outcomes at matched generation or evaluation counts, which is the standard practice in the EA literature — this implicitly assumes migration-decision overhead is negligible relative to fitness evaluation cost, an assumption that may not hold for this project's cheap-per-generation TSP fitness evaluations combined with a diversity computation that is itself O(population × edges) per epoch.

**Conclusion for Q5: this is a genuine, apparently unaddressed gap in the accessible literature.** The project's runtime-cost-vs-quality framing appears to be a novel contribution/observation rather than a replication of a known result, and should be reported as such rather than attributed to any source.

## Concrete recommendations for this project

1. **Switch the diversity/stagnation trigger from "distance to own best" to a population-wide pairwise or edge-frequency-entropy measure.** Concretely: compute edge-usage frequency f(e) across all individuals on the island, then Shannon entropy H = −Σ p(e) log p(e) over the normalized frequency distribution (following the construction described in Nagata 2020 and the entropy-based diversity-optimisation literature, arXiv:2104.13538). Re-sweep the same tau-style threshold (or an entropy-normalized threshold in [0,1]) across the same range used before (0.9→0.01) and check whether trigger rate becomes selective (target: a monotonic, well-spread trigger-rate curve rather than the near-saturated 90–100% seen with distance-to-best). *Confirming outcome*: trigger rate should show meaningfully more spread/sensitivity across the tau sweep, and should correlate with actual fitness-stagnation windows (a per-island fitness-improvement plateau) more tightly than the current metric. *Motivating source*: Nagata (2020), MIT Press Evolutionary Computation 28(4).

2. **Separately, or as a second/complementary experiment, compute mean pairwise edge distance (average over all C(30,2) pairs within an island) instead of distance-to-best.** This isolates whether the weakness is specifically "single reference point" vs. "edge-set distance metric itself." *Exact change*: replace the current O(population) distance-to-best computation with an O(population²) or a subsampled-pairs approximation of mean pairwise edge distance. *Confirming outcome*: if pairwise diversity collapses more/sooner than distance-to-best while fitness plateaus, this confirms the single-reference-point hypothesis from Q1; if it also stays high, the operator mix (crossover/mutation) is the dominant cause regardless of metric, pointing to recommendation 4 instead. *Motivating source*: Nikfarjam et al., arXiv:2104.13538 (edge diversity ED / pairwise diversity PD as the standard alternative to entropy).

3. **Run an ablation disabling Order Crossover recombination between migrated individuals and residents (or reducing crossover rate) while holding inversion mutation at 0.25**, to directly test the hypothesis (argued in Q2) that OX recombination, not inversion mutation, is the dominant source of persistent edge diversity. *Exact change*: temporarily set crossover rate to near-0 for a controlled run, keep mutation at 0.25, and re-measure whether mean-edge-distance-to-best (the existing metric) now declines over epochs. *Confirming outcome*: if diversity collapses meaningfully once crossover is removed, this confirms crossover (not mutation) as the diversity driver, which reframes the mutation-rate hypothesis in the original research question. *Motivating source*: general TSP-crossover-disruption literature (operator-mechanics argument in Q2); no single paper directly measures this for this exact configuration, so this is a project-run ablation motivated by, not replicated from, the literature.

4. **If recommendation 1 or 2 does not produce a selective trigger, treat non-collapse of diversity as a real, parameter-driven property of this configuration (per Q2/Q3) rather than a metric artifact, and switch the DTAM design away from a hard threshold toward the "soft acceptance probability" style used by Li & Wu (arXiv:1701.01271):** make migration probability a continuous, monotonic function of diversity rather than an all-or-nothing gate at tau. *Exact change*: replace `if diversity < tau: migrate` with `migrate with probability p = f(diversity)` for some monotonic f (e.g., linear or sigmoid), still evaluated every epoch. *Confirming outcome*: this should produce genuinely graded migration behavior even if the underlying diversity metric rarely dips very low, since it no longer requires crossing a hard cutoff to have any effect. *Motivating source*: Li & Wu, arXiv:1701.01271 (subpopulation-diversity-based migration-probability scheme) — noting this source's specific quantitative claims are [weak evidence/unverified] pending full-text access, but the *structural idea* (probability, not hard gate) is independently sound as a response to a saturated trigger-rate curve.

5. **To address the runtime-overhead problem (Q5, an identified literature gap):** reduce the cost of the diversity computation itself rather than its frequency semantics. *Exact change*: compute the stagnation/diversity signal from a cheap proxy — e.g., track each island's best-fitness improvement rate over the last E generations (an O(1)-per-generation counter already available from existing bookkeeping) as a fast stagnation proxy, and only fall back to the expensive full edge-distance/entropy computation on islands whose fitness-improvement proxy already suggests stagnation. *Confirming outcome*: throughput cost of the adaptive policy should drop well below the current ~30%, while retaining most of the quality benefit measured previously (targeting recovery of a *net per-second* quality advantage rather than the current 8.4% per-second loss). *Motivating source*: this is a project-specific systems optimization not directly drawn from a paper (Q5 found no literature precedent for this exact tradeoff) — justified by general algorithmic-cost reasoning (O(1) proxy vs. O(population×edges) full computation) rather than by a citation.

6. **Report the tau-sweep result (near-saturation at 90–100% trigger rate across almost the whole range) as evidence the metric, not the concept of diversity-triggered migration, is likely at fault**, and prioritize experiments 1–2 above before concluding that diversity-triggered migration is fundamentally unhelpful for this problem — the broader literature (Q4) has multiple (albeit only partially verified) reports that diversity/adaptivity-aware migration schemes can outperform fixed migration on TSP-family problems, so abandoning the DTAM concept without first fixing the metric would be premature given current evidence.
